About This Book

The Accumulation Simulator is the Risk Simulator's mirror image. Where the Risk Simulator asks "will this pot survive being drawn down," the Accumulation Simulator asks "how big could this pot realistically get in the first place." Same fan-chart machinery, same historical market data, same honest embrace of uncertainty over false precision — pointed at the other end of a working life.

It exists for a specific reason: the standard way of explaining "start saving early" is a single smooth compounding curve, and a single smooth curve is easy to nod at and forget. A spread of 10,000 real possible outcomes, with a genuine range between a disappointing run and a strong one, tends to land harder — especially with someone who isn't near retirement themselves and has every reason to believe "later" is soon enough.

This companion exists to explain how that spread is built, and how to use it well.

The Person Just Starting Out

Early twenties, first payslip, wondering whether a pension is worth the smaller take-home pay.

The Family Member Showing Them

Wants a chart that makes the case for starting early more convincingly than "trust me."

The IFA / Professional

Wants the underlying statistical model, the exact formulas, and where it deliberately simplifies.

Every chapter is built the same way, in three layers:

  1. EBOOK SECTION — the idea, in plain language, with an analogy.
  2. MANUAL SECTION — the exact UI reference: every field, slider, and button, and how to reproduce a specific test.
  3. TEACHING AID SECTION — how to use this to make the case yourself, a checklist, review questions, and a hands-on challenge.

A field name in this book and a field name on your screen are always the same string — e.g. STARTING SAVINGS (£), DESIRED RETIREMENT AGE.

Part I — Why a Fan Chart for Saving Up?

1. The Power of Compounding, Seen Honestly

1.1 Ebook Section — The "Why"

"Start early, it compounds" is true, and also one of the most under-persuasive true sentences in personal finance. It's abstract. It has no texture. A single smooth curve rising gently to the right doesn't feel like anything is actually at stake — it looks inevitable, which makes it easy to defer.

What actually happens to real money, saved by a real person, over real decades, is nothing like a smooth curve. Markets have brilliant years and dreadful ones, in no predictable order, and the same monthly contribution can land in the middle of either. Two people saving identically from the same starting age can end up with genuinely different pots, purely because of which twenty-year stretch of market history they happened to live through.

That's not a flaw to hide. It's the actual, honest shape of the future — and it's far more convincing than a single confident line, because it doesn't pretend to know something no one can know.

ANALOGY

Compare two ways of describing a long car journey. "You'll arrive at 4pm" sounds precise and is almost certainly wrong. "You'll probably arrive between 3:30 and 5, depending on traffic" is less satisfying to say, and far more useful to hear. The Accumulation Simulator is the second kind of answer.

1.2 Manual Section — The Mechanics

Every simulation, regardless of mode, follows the same loop, once per simulated year:

  1. Start with whatever's already saved — the STARTING SAVINGS figure.
  2. Apply that year's market return to the existing pot.
  3. Add that year's contribution — MONTHLY CONTRIBUTION × 12, escalated by CONTRIBUTION REAL INCREASE % compounding from the very first year.
  4. Repeat until DESIRED RETIREMENT AGE is reached; record the final pot for that one imagined lifetime.

Steps 1–3 repeat 10,000 times, each with an independently-drawn sequence of returns.

Notice what's deliberately absent: there's no separate cash bucket here, and no defensive draw threshold — those exist in the Risk Simulator because a retiree drawing an income needs a buffer against having to sell shares at a bad moment. A saver decades from retirement isn't drawing anything yet, so there's nothing to buffer against. Everything sits in one pot, growing at one rate.

TECHNICAL CALLOUT — IFA / advanced readers

The simulation runs on the same seeded pseudo-random generator as the Risk Simulator: the same starting pot, contribution schedule, mode, mean, and standard deviation always reproduce the exact same 10,000 paths, bit-for-bit. Change any input and the generator re-seeds, producing a genuinely new set of paths.

1.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • A single compounding curve looks inevitable. A spread of real outcomes looks like the truth.
  • Two identical savers can end up with meaningfully different pots, purely by chance of timing.
  • The absence of a cash bucket here isn't a missing feature — it reflects that there's nothing to draw down yet.
  • The simulation is deterministic and reproducible for a given set of inputs.

Review Questions:

  1. (Conceptual) Why might a fan chart persuade someone to start saving earlier than a single compounding line would?
  2. (Conceptual) Two people save identically from the same age. Explain how their final pots could genuinely differ.
  3. (Practical) Why doesn't this tool have a Cash Pot field, unlike the Risk Simulator?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Set Current Age to 22 and Desired Retirement Age to 65. Note the Median figure. Now change Current Age to 32, leaving everything else the same, and compare the new Median to the old one. The gap between those two numbers is the cost of waiting ten years — a concrete, current-money way to make the case.

Part II — Two Ways to Imagine the Future

2. Historical vs Parametric Mode

2.1 Ebook Section — The "Why"

There are two honest ways to guess at future market returns, and this tool offers both, side by side, so neither one gets mistaken for a guarantee.

Historical says: look at what actually happened. Take the real, recorded annual returns of the MSCI World index and draw from them at random, in effect asking "what if the next N years looked like some randomly-shuffled stretch of the real past?" It has the advantage of being grounded in something that genuinely occurred — real crashes, real recoveries, real decades of growth — rather than a mathematically convenient shape.

Parametric says: don't assume the future resembles any particular past. Instead, pick a mean return and a volatility, and draw from an invented statistical distribution built to match. It's more flexible — you can stress-test a deliberately pessimistic or optimistic assumption — but it's a model of a model, one step further from anything that actually happened.

ANALOGY

Historical mode is like predicting next winter's weather from an actual record of the last fifty winters. Parametric mode is like predicting it from a formula for "average British winter." Both are reasonable. Neither is certain. Knowing which one you're looking at matters.

2.2 Manual Section — The Mechanics

The mode toggle sits above the chart: HISTORICAL (MSCI WORLD, GBP) and PARAMETRIC FAN CHART. Switching modes changes the fields immediately below it:

  • Historical shows nothing extra — the real, embedded return sequence is used directly, the same dataset the Risk Simulator draws on.
  • Parametric reveals two fields: MEAN NOMINAL RETURN % (the average annual return the invented distribution centres on) and VOLATILITY (STDEV) % (how widely individual years swing around that average). Both apply only to the coloured fan and the Median line — never to the dashed line, which has its own independent source (see Chapter 3).

INFLATION / CPI % is always visible in both modes — it converts whatever nominal return is drawn into a real, inflation-adjusted one, since the whole chart is shown in today's money.

2.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • Historical draws from real recorded market history. Parametric draws from an invented statistical shape.
  • Neither is "more correct" — they answer slightly different questions.
  • Parametric's Mean and Volatility fields only appear in Parametric mode, and only affect the fan and Median — never the dashed line.

Review Questions:

  1. (Conceptual) What's the practical difference between drawing from real history and drawing from an invented distribution?
  2. (Practical) Switch between the two modes without changing anything else. Which figures on the page change, and which stay identical? Why?

MINI-CHALLENGE

In Parametric mode, set Volatility to 0%. Every one of the 10,000 paths becomes identical, so Poor, Median, and Strong should all read the exact same number. If they don't, something's wrong — this is a genuine, precise way to sanity-check the tool yourself.

Part III — Contributions & the Assumed Rate

3. Monthly Contributions, Real Increase, and the Independent Assumed Rate Line

3.1 Ebook Section — The "Why"

Two things drive a growing pot: what the market does to money already invested, and what keeps getting added to it. The fan and Median line answer the first question — a genuine spread of 10,000 market outcomes. The dashed line answers a completely different question: "if growth were exactly this one flat rate, with no randomness at all, where would the pot land?" It's a ruler laid across the chart, not another guess at the future.

Keeping these genuinely separate matters. Early in this tool's life, the dashed line and the Parametric mean were the same slider — which meant you couldn't hold a steady "for reference" assumption while exploring different Parametric scenarios. They're independent now, on purpose, mirroring exactly how the Risk Simulator already separates its own Assumed Real Growth Rate from its Parametric fields.

ANALOGY

Think of the dashed line as a straightedge held up against a wandering coastline on a map. It doesn't pretend the coastline is straight — it just gives you something steady to compare the wandering against.

3.2 Manual Section — The Mechanics

  • MONTHLY CONTRIBUTION (£) — paid in every month, added to the pot annually as contribution × 12.
  • CONTRIBUTION REAL INCREASE % — an annual uplift applied to that contribution, compounding from the first year. A saver whose contributions rise with their salary, in other words, not a saver stuck at one flat amount forever.
  • SHOWN ON CHART AS ⋯ ASSUMED REAL GROWTH RATE % — always visible regardless of mode, this slider alone drives the dashed line. It never affects the coloured fan or the Median line, and Parametric's Mean/Volatility fields never affect it back.

TECHNICAL CALLOUT — IFA / advanced readers

The Assumed Real Growth Rate slider is treated internally as a nominal rate and converted to real terms via the same inflation-adjustment formula used throughout Sovereign Glidepath — a genuinely different calculation path from the Parametric mean, not just a different slider showing the same number.

3.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • The dashed line is a fixed reference rate, not a 10,000-path simulation.
  • It's fully independent from Parametric's Mean and Volatility — moving one never moves the other.
  • Contribution Real Increase % models rising contributions, not a flat amount forever.

Review Questions:

  1. (Practical) Change the Assumed Real Growth Rate slider. Which line on the chart moves, and which stay put?
  2. (Conceptual) Why might someone want the dashed line to stay fixed while they experiment with Parametric's Mean and Volatility?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Set the Assumed Real Growth Rate equal to Parametric's Mean Nominal Return, and set Volatility to 0% and Inflation to 0%. The dashed line and the Median line should now sit exactly on top of each other — visual proof that two separately-coded calculations agree when their inputs genuinely match.

Part IV — Reading the Fan

4. Percentiles, the Crosshair Tooltip, and the Zoom Brush

4.1 Ebook Section — The "Why"

Ten thousand simulated lifetimes can't all be shown on one chart — the shaded band and three lines are a deliberate summary, not a shortcut. The band's top and bottom edges are the 90th and 10th percentiles: at any given age, 90% of simulated paths sit below the top edge, and 10% sit below the bottom edge. The blue line through the middle is the Median — the exact midpoint outcome, with as many paths finishing above it as below.

Reading that as "worst case to best case" is a common and understandable mistake. It isn't — a true worst case would mean a total loss, and a true best case is unbounded. Think of the band instead as "the middle 80% of plausible outcomes," which is a genuinely different, more honest claim.

ANALOGY

A weather forecaster's "70% chance of rain" isn't a guarantee of rain, and it isn't a guarantee of sun either — it's a statement about how often outcomes like this one tend to include rain. The percentile band works the same way.

4.2 Manual Section — The Mechanics

  • Hover anywhere on the chart and a crosshair appears with a tooltip showing the exact age, and the Assumed Rate / 90th percentile / Median / 10th percentile values at that precise point.
  • The zoom brush beneath the chart lets you drag either handle to narrow the visible age range, or drag the highlighted window itself to slide it — useful for looking closely at the early, lower-value years without the later, larger numbers compressing everything into a thin strip. Double-click the brush to reset to the full range.
  • The summary row below the chart repeats the final-age figures in plain text: Pot at [age] — Poor (p10), Median (p50), Strong (p90), and Total contributed over the whole period.

4.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • The band is the middle 80% of outcomes, not a worst-to-best range.
  • The tooltip gives exact figures at any age, not just the final one.
  • The zoom brush is for looking closely, not for changing any input.

Review Questions:

  1. (Conceptual) Why is it wrong to describe the top of the band as "the best case"?
  2. (Practical) How would you use the zoom brush to inspect the pot's value in someone's late twenties specifically?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Zoom into just the first five years using the brush. Hover across them and watch how close together Poor, Median, and Strong still are — the spread widens with time, because small early differences compound into large later ones. Double-click to reset when done.

Part V — Remembering Where You Left Off

5. Persistence — Every Field Is Sticky

5.1 Ebook Section — The "Why"

The Risk Simulator has a real plan behind it — Pane 1's live figures — so it only remembers a handful of what-if tuning fields, and always seeds the rest fresh from your actual plan. The Accumulation Simulator has no such anchor. There's no "real plan" to fall back to, so instead every single field remembers itself: leave the tab, come back, and everything is exactly as you left it.

5.2 Manual Section — The Mechanics

Every field on this page — ages, savings, contributions, mode, rates, and the fields in the hand-off window covered in Chapter 6 — is saved automatically as you edit it. The one exception is deliberate: if a value arrives via the page's URL (for instance, a currency passed in from elsewhere), that URL value takes precedence for that one field, over whatever was previously remembered. Everything else falls back to the last value you entered, and only then to a sensible starting default.

TECHNICAL CALLOUT — IFA / advanced readers

Values are stored in the browser's own local storage, scoped to this page only — nothing is sent to a server, and nothing here is shared with or written back to the main Sovereign Glidepath app or the Risk Simulator. Clearing your browser's site data will reset it to defaults.

5.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • Every field remembers itself — there's no "real plan" here to reset back to.
  • A value arriving via the page's link takes priority over what was previously remembered, for that one field only.
  • Nothing here is sent anywhere or shared with the rest of the app.

Review Questions:

  1. (Practical) You set Current Age to 25 and close the tab. What will Current Age show the next time you open this page?
  2. (Conceptual) Why does this tool need to remember everything, when the Risk Simulator only remembers a few fields?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Change several fields, close the tab entirely, then reopen this page from a fresh link with no parameters. Confirm your changes are still there — you've verified the persistence yourself.

Part VI — Moving to the Risk Simulator

6. The Hand-off Window

6.1 Ebook Section — The "Why"

Building a pot and living off one are different problems, with different risks — which is why this tool and the Risk Simulator are separate apps in the first place. But they're two ends of the same story, and seeing how a projected pot actually holds up against a real retirement — inflation, sequence-of-returns risk, a State Pension arriving partway through — is a natural next question once you've watched it grow.

The Move this pot to the Risk Simulator button is that bridge. It doesn't just copy a number across — it asks a few short questions, shows you exactly what it's about to hand over, and only acts once you confirm.

6.2 Manual Section — The Mechanics

Clicking the button opens a single confirmation window containing four fields, followed by a live review:

  • DESIRED ANNUAL DRAWDOWN (£) — the yearly income wanted once retired.
  • STATE PENSION AGE — when State Pension is expected to start.
  • STATE PENSION AMOUNT (£) — the expected yearly State Pension, in today's money.
  • Starting pot taken from — a choice between the projected Median outcome or the Assumed Growth Rate (dashed) line's final value, as the figure that becomes the Risk Simulator's starting capital.

Below those fields, the review table updates live as you edit anything above it:

Row Where it comes from
Starting Pot Whichever line was chosen above, at your Desired Retirement Age.
Equities (85%) / Cash (15%) The Starting Pot, split in a fixed 85/15 ratio.
Age Your Desired Retirement Age here becomes the Risk Simulator's Current Age.
Horizon Age State Pension Age + 25 — a sensible default, freely editable once you arrive.
Annual Withdrawal Desired Annual Drawdown, unchanged.
State Pension Start Age / Amount The two State Pension fields above, unchanged.

Cancel closes the window with no effect at all. Open Risk Simulator opens it in a new tab, pre-filled with everything above. Nothing is written back to this page, and nothing on this page is written back to the Risk Simulator or the main app — the two tools stay independent sandboxes, connected only by this one deliberate hand-off.

Once there, the Risk Simulator's own "Back to…" link correctly reads Back to Accumulation Simulator rather than the usual "Back to Sovereign Glidepath" — it always reflects wherever that tab actually came from.

WHY 85/15, AND WHY STATE PENSION AGE + 25?

Both are starting points, not rules — chosen to be reasonable defaults for someone who hasn't thought about asset allocation or life expectancy yet, not a claim that everyone should hold exactly that split or plan to exactly that age. Every figure the hand-off produces, including these two, is fully editable the moment you land in the Risk Simulator.

6.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • The hand-off is a one-way, deliberate action — nothing happens until you confirm.
  • The review updates live as you edit the four fields, before anything is sent anywhere.
  • The 85/15 split and the Horizon Age default are starting points, not fixed rules — both are editable afterward.
  • The Risk Simulator's back-link correctly reflects that it was opened from here.

Review Questions:

  1. (Practical) Switch the "Starting pot taken from" choice between Median and Assumed Growth Rate. What changes in the review table, and what doesn't?
  2. (Conceptual) Why might Horizon Age be based on State Pension Age rather than Desired Retirement Age?
  3. (Practical) After opening the Risk Simulator this way, how do you get back to the Accumulation Simulator, and what does the link actually say?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Open the hand-off window, set a Desired Annual Drawdown, State Pension Age and Amount, and confirm. In the Risk Simulator, check that the Equities and Cash figures really do sum to the Starting Pot you saw in the review table, and that they're in roughly an 85/15 ratio.

Master Answer Key

Chapter 1

  1. A spread of real possible outcomes reflects genuine uncertainty rather than a false, tidy promise — which tends to land as more credible, not less.
  2. Identical contributions can land in different market years by chance, so the same monthly amount compounds differently depending purely on timing.
  3. There's nothing to draw down yet during accumulation, so there's no need for a defensive cash buffer — that concept belongs to the drawdown phase.

Chapter 2

  1. Historical draws from real recorded market history; Parametric draws from an invented statistical shape built to a chosen Mean and Volatility.
  2. Total Contributed and the dashed Assumed Rate line stay identical — neither depends on which mode is selected. The fan and Median change, since they depend on the market model.

Chapter 3

  1. Only the dashed line moves. The coloured fan and Median line are untouched, since they're driven by the mode's own Mean/Volatility or historical draw.
  2. A fixed reference point lets you compare different Parametric assumptions against one steady baseline, rather than the baseline itself shifting every time you experiment.

Chapter 4

  1. The band's top isn't a ceiling — it's the 90th percentile, meaning 10% of simulated paths finish even higher than that.
  2. Drag the left handle of the zoom brush inward to shrink the visible range down to roughly ages 22–30 (or the relevant window), leaving the right handle in place.

Chapter 5

  1. It will still show 25 — every field on this page is remembered automatically.
  2. The Risk Simulator has a real plan behind it from Pane 1 to fall back on; this tool has no such anchor, so everything here needs to be sticky.

Chapter 6

  1. The Starting Pot figure changes, and so do the Equities/Cash figures derived from it — everything else in the table stays the same.
  2. Desired Retirement Age can be very young, which would make a poor anchor for how long the money needs to last; State Pension Age is a steadier reference point for that.
  3. Click the "← Back to Accumulation Simulator" link at the top of the Risk Simulator page — it correctly names where that tab came from.
