About This Book

The Risk Simulator is the most statistically dense screen in Sovereign Glidepath. It has more moving parts than any other single screen in the app, and it is the pane most likely to be misread — not because it is poorly built, but because it asks you to hold several genuinely different ideas in your head at once: randomness, compounding, sequence risk, and the mechanics of a two-bucket withdrawal engine, all overlaid on one chart.

This companion exists to separate those ideas out, one at a time, and put them back together properly.

The Complete Beginner

New to pensions, new to investing — wants the plain-English version first.

The Experienced DIYer

Grew a pot for decades, now nervous about drawing it down and sequence-of-returns risk.

The IFA / Professional

Wants the underlying statistical model, the exact formulas, and the assumptions.

Every chapter is built the same way, in three layers:

  1. EBOOK SECTION — the idea, in plain language, with an analogy.
  2. MANUAL SECTION — the exact UI reference: every field, slider, and button, and how to reproduce a specific test.
  3. TEACHING AID SECTION — how to use this in your own plan, a checklist, review questions, and a hands-on challenge.

A field name in this book and a field name on your screen are always the same string — e.g. GLOBAL EQUITIES POT £, PARAMETRIC EXPECTED ANNUAL EQUITY RETURN %.

Part I — Why Simulate At All?

1. Sequence-of-Returns Risk & the Case for 10,000 Futures

1.1 Ebook Section — The "Why"

Suppose two people each retire with £500,000, and each plans to draw £20,000 a year for 20 years. Over those 20 years the stock market delivers exactly the same set of annual returns to both of them — a few great years, a few terrible years, mostly average years. The average return is identical for both.

Yet one of them can end up rich, and the other can run out of money entirely.

How? The order the returns arrive in matters, not just the average. If two brutal down years hit right at the start of retirement, bills still have to be paid, forcing shares to be sold while prices are low — locking in the loss permanently, before any recovery can help. If those same two brutal years arrive at the very end instead, 18 good years have already built a cushion, and the bad years barely dent it.

Same average return. Wildly different outcomes. This is called sequence-of-returns risk, and it is arguably the single most important, least intuitive concept in retirement planning.

ANALOGY

A weather forecaster doesn't say "18°C tomorrow, guaranteed." They say "likely around 18°C, could range 14–22°C." The Risk Simulator does the same for your retirement — trading false precision for honest uncertainty.

1.2 Manual Section — The Mechanics

Every simulation in the Risk Simulator, regardless of mode, follows the same loop:

  1. Start with your Equities Pot and Cash Pot at their current values.
  2. For each year (or quarter — see Chapter 4), generate a return for that period.
  3. Apply growth, then the withdrawal (netted against any pension in payment), sourced from whichever bucket the Defensive Draw Threshold dictates.
  4. Re-check the Guyton-Klinger guardrail and adjust by ±10% if triggered.
  5. Repeat to your Target Horizon Age; record the final pot size for that one imagined lifetime.

Steps 1–5 repeat 10,000 times, each with an independently-drawn sequence of returns.

TECHNICAL CALLOUT — IFA / advanced readers

The simulation runs on a seeded mulberry32 pseudo-random generator: the same starting pot, years, mode, mean, and standard deviation always reproduce the exact same 10,000 paths, bit-for-bit. Changing any of those five inputs re-seeds the generator and produces a genuinely new set of paths.

1.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • Average return tells you almost nothing about whether your specific plan survives.
  • The order of good/bad years can make or break an otherwise identical plan.
  • The Risk Simulator makes this hidden risk visible by running many possible orderings.
  • The simulation is deterministic and reproducible for a given set of inputs.

Review Questions:

  1. (Conceptual) Two retirees have identical pots, plans, and average returns over 20 years. Explain why one could end up broke while the other ends up wealthy.
  2. (Conceptual) What is sequence-of-returns risk, and why is it more dangerous early in retirement?
  3. (Practical) If you run the simulation twice without changing inputs, will the fan chart differ? Why or why not?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Part II — Two Ways to Imagine the Future

2. Historical vs Parametric Mode

2.1 Ebook Section

When the Risk Simulator needs a return for a simulated year, it gets that number one of two ways. Historical mode reaches into real market history — a table of actual MSCI World (GBP) annual returns, 1970–2024, 55 real years. Each simulated year, for each of the 10,000 paths, the engine picks one of those 55 real years at random. Parametric mode instead invents an imaginary market from just two numbers you choose: an average return, and a volatility (how wildly returns swing). It draws from a smooth mathematical bell curve shaped by those two numbers.

ANALOGY

Historical mode is shuffling 55 real playing cards, each with a real year's return, and dealing one at random each year. Parametric mode is inventing your own deck from a formula.

Neither is "more correct" — Historical asks "what if the future rhymes with the past?" Parametric asks "what if the future matches this specific assumption?"

2.2 Manual Section — Fields & Effects

Control Effect
Historical (MSCI World, GBP) Draws real 1970–2024 annual returns at random, with replacement, for every simulated year across all 10,000 paths.
Parametric Generates returns from a Normal distribution using the two fields below.
PARAMETRIC EXPECTED ANNUAL EQUITY RETURN % The mean of the invented distribution (nominal). Affects only the 10,000 stochastic paths — no effect on the dashed Assumed Rate line (Chapter 6).
PARAMETRIC RETURN VOLATILITY (STANDARD DEVIATION) % How widely returns swing around the mean. Higher = wider fan, bigger gap between median and dashed line. Also no effect on the dashed line.

TECHNICAL CALLOUT

Historical draws are made with replacement from a fixed 55-element array. Parametric draws use a Box–Muller transform: nominal = mean + sd × Z, with Z drawn fresh every year of every path.

Reproducible test: Note the Median Ending Value under Historical mode, then switch to Parametric with mean ≈11.6% and SD ≈17.1% (the 1970–2024 series' own arithmetic statistics). The fans should look broadly similar in shape, though never identical — Historical mode's real sequence has clustering structure a smooth curve doesn't replicate.

2.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • Historical mode = real market history, reshuffled at random.
  • Parametric mode = an invented market, shaped only by a mean and volatility you choose.
  • The Parametric fields affect only the fan and median — never the dashed line.

Review Questions:

  1. (Conceptual) Why might Historical mode produce more "clumpy" bad patches than Parametric mode?
  2. (Practical) Which two fields only appear in Parametric mode?
  3. (Practical) If you raise the Parametric mean from 6% to 10%, what happens to the dashed line?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Run your plan in Historical mode, note the Ruin Rate. Switch to Parametric with mean/SD ≈11.6%/17.1%, compare. Explain any difference using what you now know about sequence structure.

Part III — The Two-Bucket Engine

3. Equities, Cash, and the Defensive Draw Threshold

3.1 Ebook Section

Imagine two jars: a big, volatile jar (Equities) that grows faster long-term but can lose value suddenly, and a small, steady jar (Cash) that barely grows but never crashes. Always paying bills from the volatile jar means sometimes selling shares at exactly the worst moment. The smarter approach: pay bills from Cash in good years (topping Cash back up from Equities' gains), and pay entirely from Cash in bad years, letting Equities recover untouched. The Risk Simulator's two-bucket engine does this automatically, every simulated period, across all 10,000 futures.

3.2 Manual Section — Fields & Effects

Control Effect
GLOBAL EQUITIES POT £ / CASH POT £ Starting bucket sizes for the simulation. These are independent copies of Pane 1 — they do not auto-sync. Watch for the ✎ WHAT-IF badge.
✎ WHAT-IF badge + "Reset to actual (£X)" Warns a field no longer matches your real, current recorded plan. Click the link to restore it.
Allocation Bias slider Actively rebalances your pots — dragging it computes newEquities = total × (slider % ÷ 100) and newCash = total − newEquities, and writes both values back immediately. It holds your total pot fixed but genuinely moves money between the two buckets. This is not a passive read-out.
Strict / Standard / Aggressive How readily the simulation switches to cash-first spending. Strict: only a genuinely bad year (real return below ≈−5%). Standard: flat or weakly positive years (below half your Assumed Real Growth Rate). Aggressive: almost any year that isn't clearly strong (below the full Assumed Real Growth Rate).
ASSUMED REAL GROWTH RATE % (Equities) Sets the Defensive Draw Threshold's reference point. Does not directly grow Equities inside the fan/median — see Chapter 6.
CASH REAL RETURN % The real annual growth genuinely applied to the Cash Pot in every one of the 10,000 paths — a direct, continuous effect.

KNOWN QUIRK — "Reset split to actual" resets both pots together

When the Allocation Bias slider has been moved, a "Reset split to actual" link appears. Clicking it resets both the Equities and Cash fields back to their live/actual values in one action — even if you had separately and deliberately overridden just one of the two fields for an unrelated reason using its own individual ✎ WHAT-IF reset link. In effect, this button behaves as a small global reset rather than undoing only the bias-slider change. It is a minor, low-impact quirk — but worth knowing before you rely on a carefully-constructed what-if scenario that mixes an individual pot override with a bias-slider tweak.

TECHNICAL CALLOUT

The threshold hurdle: Strict = fixed −5%; Standard = half your Assumed Real Growth Rate; Aggressive = the full Assumed Real Growth Rate. Below the hurdle, that period is funded from Cash (subject to Cash actually covering it); otherwise, spending comes from Equities with any surplus swept into Cash up to its starting target size.

Reproducible test: With Assumed Real Growth Rate at 4% and Standard selected (hurdle 2%), any simulated year with real equity return below 2% is funded from Cash. Compare Ruin Rate and the 10th-percentile floor between Strict and Standard on identical inputs — Standard should show a firmer floor.

3.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • Equities and Cash are genuinely separate buckets, not one blended pot.
  • The Defensive Draw Threshold decides, each period, which bucket funds spending.
  • The Allocation Bias slider is an active control — it rebalances real pot values, holding the total fixed.
  • Its "Reset split to actual" link resets both pots together — a minor but real global-reset quirk.
  • The Equities/Cash Pot fields can silently drift from your real plan — always check for ✎ WHAT-IF.

Review Questions:

  1. (Conceptual) Why might always spending from Equities during a crash damage a plan more than spending from Cash during that same crash?
  2. (Practical) You change Pane 1's Equities Pot from £500,000 to £600,000. Will the Risk Simulator automatically reflect this? How would you check?
  3. (Practical) Under which threshold setting would a flat (0% real return) year most readily trigger cash-first spending?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Drag the Allocation Bias slider and confirm both the Equities and Cash fields change together, with the total held fixed. Then click "Reset split to actual" and confirm both fields snap back — even if you'd only meant to undo the slider.

Part IV — The Guardrail in Motion

4. Tick Mode & Guyton-Klinger

4.1 Ebook Section

A fixed withdrawal — draw exactly £X every year regardless of markets — is fragile. The Guyton-Klinger method adjusts dynamically: if your withdrawal rate has drifted more than 20% above where you started, cut it by 10% ("Preservation"). If it's drifted more than 20% below a baseline, you may raise it by 10% ("Prosperity"). Otherwise, draw normally. It's a plan that bends under pressure rather than breaking.

4.2 Manual Section — Fields & Effects

Control Effect
Yearly tick One G-K check, growth step, and withdrawal per year.
Quarterly tick Four checks/growth-steps/withdrawals per year, each year's return split into four equal geometric quarters. Matches how the live app behaves committing a ledger row each quarter. Expect a slightly firmer p10 floor than Yearly, on identical inputs.
Guyton-Klinger check (automatic) Fires every tick. Cuts 10% ("Preservation") if withdrawal rate rose >20% above the all-time-high-anchored reference; raises 10% ("Prosperity") if it fell >20% below the plan's baseline reference; else "Normal".

TECHNICAL CALLOUT — IFA / advanced readers

Preservation and Prosperity are checked against different reference rates. Preservation compares to the rate implied by the portfolio's per-path all-time high. Prosperity compares to a fixed baseline set at inception. This is deliberate: anchoring both to the all-time-high would make Prosperity nearly impossible to trigger, since a portfolio's value can never exceed its own tracked high by construction. Additionally, from age 86 onward (the No-Go phase), the guardrail switches off entirely and withdrawals proceed at the baseline rate without further ±10% adjustment.

Reproducible test: Run identical inputs under Yearly and Quarterly tick. Compare the 10th percentile — Quarterly should show a modestly higher floor, the quantifiable value of more frequent guardrail discipline.

4.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • Guyton-Klinger dynamically cuts or boosts withdrawals by 10% based on how far the withdrawal rate has drifted.
  • Preservation (cuts) and Prosperity (boosts) use two different reference points, by design.
  • Quarterly tick re-checks four times as often and typically produces a slightly safer worst case.
  • The guardrail switches off entirely late in the plan (No-Go phase, 86+).

Review Questions:

  1. (Conceptual) Why does a never-adjusted withdrawal create more risk in a bad market than a dynamic one?
  2. (Conceptual) Why are Preservation and Prosperity checked against different reference points?
  3. (Practical) Switching Yearly → Quarterly tick, what direction would you expect the 10th percentile to move?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Run your plan under both tick modes, record the Ruin Rate for each. Which is lower, and does it match this chapter's prediction?

Part V — Pensions & the WHAT-IF Trap

5. Pensions, Withdrawals, and Independent Overrides

5.1 Ebook Section

A pension changes everything, because it arrives regardless of markets. Before it starts, every pound spent comes from your pot. After it starts, your pot only needs to cover the gap. This matters for sequence risk: the years before pension start are exactly the years where a bad market sequence does the most permanent damage, since there's no other income cushioning the blow yet.

5.2 Manual Section — Fields & Effects

Control Effect
ANNUAL PENSION £ (today's £) Pension amount netted off withdrawal once Pension Start Age is reached.
PENSION START AGE Age netting begins; before this, full withdrawal comes from capital.
PENSION REAL INCREASE % Annual real-terms growth of the pension once in payment. 0% = flat-real.
PENSION DATA: Actual Pension Reads pension figures live from Pane 1 — the default, typical mode.
PENSION DATA: Hypothetical Freely edit pension figures in this pane only, without touching Pane 1.
ANNUAL WITHDRAWAL £ ("What-if only") Withdrawal used by the simulation; mirrors Pane 1's Initial Annual Withdrawal — Frozen Baseline by default but can be independently overridden here without altering your real recorded plan.

THE WHAT-IF TRAP

Both the Equities/Cash Pot fields (Chapter 3) and the pension fields in Hypothetical mode can silently drift from your real plan. Always check for ✎ WHAT-IF before trusting a result.

Reproducible test: Compare Ruin Rate with pension start age at your real age vs. five years later. The delayed version should show a meaningfully higher ruin rate — more years fully funded from capital before any pension cushion arrives.

5.3 Teaching Aid Section

Key takeaways:

  • A pension reduces the amount your pot needs to fund, from the moment it starts.
  • Years before pension start are the highest-sequence-risk years in the whole plan.
  • "Actual Pension" reads live from Pane 1; "Hypothetical" lets you experiment freely.
  • Always check ✎ WHAT-IF on Equities/Cash and pension fields before trusting a result.

Review Questions:

  1. (Conceptual) Why are the years immediately before pension start often the riskiest in a drawdown plan?
  2. (Practical) What's the difference between "Actual Pension" and "Hypothetical" mode?
  3. (Practical) You want to test a 2-year-delayed pension start without touching your real plan — which mode, and why?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Using Hypothetical mode, test three pension start ages (real, +2, +5 years), everything else constant. Record Ruin Rate for each and rank safest to riskiest.

Part VI — Reading the Fan

6. Percentiles, the Assumed Rate Line, Volatility Drag, and Skew

6.1 Ebook Section

Once 10,000 lifetimes are run, the Risk Simulator shows them via a small number of representative lines and bands:

  • The median (solid line): the outcome exactly in the middle — the typical result, not the average.
  • 10th–90th percentile band (lighter): 80% of all 10,000 futures fall inside this band at each age.
  • 25th–75th percentile band (darker): a tighter, more "reasonably likely" middle 50%.
  • The dashed Assumed Rate line: zero randomness — one perfectly smooth, identical return every year, forever. A ruler, not a prediction.

Why does the median usually sit below the dashed line? Volatility drag. £100 growing 20% then falling 20% ends at £96, not flat, even though the average of +20%/−20% is 0%. Real compounding with real ups and downs is mathematically worse than the simple average suggests — and bigger swings mean a bigger gap.

Why is the top of the fan wider than the bottom? A pot cannot fall below zero, but there's no ceiling on growth. Bad-luck outcomes are squeezed into a limited space; good-luck outcomes can keep compounding without limit. An honest, expected shape — not a charting quirk.

6.2 Manual Section — Fields, Statistics & Effects

Element / statistic Meaning
SIMS BEATING YOUR ASSUMPTION % of the 10,000 paths whose ending value exceeds the dashed line's ending value.
MEDIAN ENDING VALUE The middle outcome's final pot size, today's pounds.
10TH – 90TH RANGE Final pot sizes at the 10th and 90th percentile.
CAPITAL PRESERVED % of paths ending at or above starting capital.
RUIN RATE % of paths fully exhausted before the horizon ends.
Zoom/brush strip Drag handles to zoom an age window; Y-axis auto-rescales; double-click resets.

THE MOST IMPORTANT DISTINCTION IN THIS PANE

The Parametric mean/SD fields affect only the fan and median. Assumed Real Growth Rate (Equities) affects only the dashed line plus the Defensive Draw Threshold hurdle — it does not grow Equities within the 10,000 paths. Cash Real Return, by contrast, directly and continuously affects the fan/median in both modes. Three similar-looking fields, three genuinely different jobs.

TECHNICAL CALLOUT

Percentiles use linear interpolation on the sorted 10,000-value array: position = (N−1) × q, interpolating between bracketing values. The right-skew reflects a log-normal-shaped outcome distribution, bounded at zero on the downside, unbounded on the upside.

6.3 Teaching Aid Section — Practical Application

  1. Start with Ruin Rate — your headline safety statistic.
  2. Check Capital Preserved next.
  3. Compare median to the dashed line — a gap is volatility drag doing its job, not a red flag.
  4. Look at the fan's shape, not just the median — a wide lower half signals high sensitivity to bad luck.
  5. Stress-test deliberately: Strict vs Aggressive threshold, Yearly vs Quarterly tick, delayed pension start.

Key takeaways:

  • Median = middle outcome, not average, and not the dashed line.
  • The dashed line has zero randomness and is not a prediction.
  • Median below the dashed line is volatility drag — expected, not an error.
  • The fan is naturally wider on the upside because losses are capped at zero and gains aren't.

Review Questions:

  1. (Conceptual) Explain why a pot swinging +20%/−20% ends worse off than one that stayed flat, without using the term "volatility drag".
  2. (Conceptual) Why is the top of the fan wider than the bottom?
  3. (Practical) Which field controls the dashed line, and which two control only the fan/median in Parametric mode?

MINI-CHALLENGE

Appendix B — Glossary

  • Sequence-of-returns risk: the risk that the order of returns affects the outcome, even with an identical average.
  • Median: the middle value of a sorted set of outcomes — not the same as the average.
  • Percentile band: a range showing where a given proportion of outcomes fall.
  • Volatility drag: the effect by which a volatile series compounds to a lower typical outcome than its simple average suggests.
  • Guyton-Klinger guardrail: a rule cutting withdrawals 10% when the rate rises too far above target, permitting a 10% raise when it falls too far below a baseline.
  • Defensive Draw Threshold: the rule deciding whether a period's spending comes from Equities or Cash.
  • WHAT-IF badge: a warning that a Risk Simulator field no longer matches your real, current recorded plan.
  • Ruin Rate: the % of simulated futures where the pot is fully exhausted before the horizon ends.
  • Capital Preserved: the % of simulated futures ending at or above starting capital.

Chapter 4

Chapter 5
